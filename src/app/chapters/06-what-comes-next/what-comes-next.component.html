The next one

It will come back. What it brings is changing.

  1. 01

    ENSO stays in charge. It will very likely remain the dominant swing in the world's year-to-year climate through this century.

  2. 02

    The rain swings grow. The floods and droughts tied to El Niño and La Niña are very likely to intensify by the second half of the century, whether or not the ocean swings themselves get bigger.

  3. 03

    The floor keeps rising. As long as the ocean warms, each El Niño adds its heat to a higher starting point, and the records it helps set keep climbing.

Findings 01 and 02 summarise the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group I (2021). Finding 03 follows from the record above.

The Pacific will turn warm again. Watching for it, and knowing what it tends to bring and to whom, is the most useful long-range forecast we have.

How these charts were made
  • Sea temperatures: NOAA ERSST v5, monthly, 2° grid. Anomalies against 1991–2020 unless noted.
  • El Niño and La Niña episodes follow NOAA CPC's Oceanic Niño Index rule: a 3-month mean of the Niño 3.4 anomaly at or beyond ±0.5 °C for at least five months, against 30-year base periods re-centred every five years so that long-term warming isn't mistaken for El Niño.
  • Rainfall: NOAA GPCP v2.3, monthly, 2.5° grid, Dec–Feb averages over each region, as a percent of the 1991–2020 normal.
  • Episode fingerprints: peak 3-month Niño 3.4, Niño 1+2 and Niño 4 anomalies; tropical Pacific area at least 1 °C anomalous; tropical Indian Ocean anomaly up to six months after each episode.
  • Impact regions in chapter 4 are simplified from NOAA CPC's ENSO impact maps.
  • All data is computed by scripts/preprocess.py in this project's repository; summary numbers in the text are derived from that data as the page loads.
